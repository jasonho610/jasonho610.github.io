The filterbank on the front page

15 November 2026

The six glowing curves at the top of this site are not decoration, and they are not a waveform. They are the magnitude responses of six bandpass filters, drawn across a logarithmic frequency axis that runs from 55 Hz to about 11 kHz.

Each curve is one filter. Its peak sits at the frequency that filter lets through most freely, and the width of the hump is how tolerant it is of neighbouring frequencies — in DSP terms, its Q. The six peaks are an octave apart: 110, 220, 440, 880, 1760, 3520 Hz. That is A2 up to A7, which means the spacing is musical before it is technical. Doubling the frequency is the same move whether you call it an octave or a factor of two.

Why it is clickable

Press hear the filterbank and the page makes noise — literally. A short loop of noise, weighted a little toward the low end, is fed through six real bandpass filters in the browser’s audio graph. Their output gains open one at a time, low to high, and then all together. The curves you see are driven by analysers sitting on those same filters, so the picture and the sound are the same object seen two ways.

The reason to make it audible is that a filterbank is one of those ideas everyone can describe and few people have actually heard isolated. Opening one band at a time makes the point immediately: each band carries a recognisably different part of the same signal, and none of them sounds like the whole.

What this has to do with the research

In What Do Neural Networks Listen To? I replaced the encoder of a speech enhancement model with a Sinc-convolution, where every filter is parameterised by a learnable low and high cutoff frequency plus a band gain. The network still learns, but now its frequency selectivity is written down in two numbers per filter instead of being buried in an arbitrary convolution kernel.

So you can simply plot what the model decided to listen to. The filterbank at the top of this page is the handmade version of that figure: I chose the six centre frequencies, because octaves are a reasonable guess for how to divide up sound. A trained Sinc encoder does not need my guess. It picks its own bands, and the interesting result is that where it puts them — and which ones it turns up — tells you something about the task that the loss value alone never does.

That is more or less the thread through everything else here. A number tells you whether a model worked. A band tells you what it used.
